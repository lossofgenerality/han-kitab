## 1. What the Han Kitab is

**Han Kitab** (漢克塔布 *Hàn kètǎbù*; Arabic/Persian هان کتاب) is the name Chinese Muslims gave to the body of Islamic books written **in Classical Chinese** by Chinese-speaking Muslims (today's Hui 回族), mainly between **1642 and the late 19th century**. *Han* means "Chinese"; *kitāb* is Arabic for "book".

These books explain Islamic doctrine, Sufi metaphysics, ritual law and the life of the Prophet in the vocabulary of **Song–Ming Neo-Confucianism** (理 *li*, 氣 *qi*, 性 *xing*, 太極 *taiji*, 無極 *wuji*), with borrowings from Buddhist and Daoist language. Chinese scholarship calls the method **以儒詮經** *yi Ru quan jing*, "interpreting the (Islamic) scriptures through Confucianism", and its authors **回儒** *Huiru*, "Muslim Confucians".

Three features define the corpus:

- **Language:** literary Chinese, so that Muslims who no longer read Arabic or Persian could study their faith, and so that Confucian officials and literati could read it.
- **Sources:** mostly Persian and Arabic works of the madrasa and Sufi curriculum (Nasafī, Najm al-Dīn Rāzī's *Mirṣād al-ʿibād*, Jāmī's *Lawāʾiḥ* and *Ashiʿʿat al-lamaʿāt*, ʿAzīz Nasafī's *Maqṣad-i aqṣā*, Hanafi law books).
- **Method:** not a replacement of Islam by Confucianism, but an argument that Islam's teaching on God, the cosmos, the human being and the five relationships (五倫) is the fulfilment of what the Confucian sages taught.

The main centres were **Nanjing (Jinling 金陵)**, **Suzhou**, **Yangzhou**, **Beijing**, **Jining** and, in the 19th century, **Yunnan** (Kunming, Dali).

## 2. Background: Muslims in China before the Han Kitab

| Period | Events |
|---|---|
| **Tang (618–907)** | Chinese and Muslim tradition dates the first Arab embassy to **651** (Yonghui 2). Arab and Persian merchants settle in the "foreign quarters" (蕃坊 *fanfang*) of Guangzhou, Quanzhou, Yangzhou and Chang'an. Islam is the faith of foreigners, not yet of Chinese subjects. |
| **Song (960–1279)** | Families born in China (土生蕃客 "native-born foreign guests") intermarry and some sit the civil examinations. |
| **Yuan (1271–1368)** | Under the Mongols, Central Asian and Persian Muslims enter China in large numbers as **色目** *semu* ("various categories") officials, soldiers and artisans, above the Han in the legal order. Sayyid Ajall Shams al-Dīn governs Yunnan. The term **回回** *Huihui* comes to mean "Muslims". Chinese-language mosque inscriptions appear (Quanzhou *Qingjing si ji* 清淨寺記, Guangzhou *Chongjian Huaisheng si ji* 重建懷聖寺記, both 1350). |
| **Ming (1368–1644)** | The Hongwu emperor restores Han rule. The Ming code requires Mongols and *semu* to marry Chinese rather than among themselves; Muslims take Chinese surnames (Ma 馬, Hai 海, Na 納, Sa 薩…), dress and speech. Within a few generations most Huihui speak only Chinese. Muslims serve the state (Zheng He 鄭和, 1371–1433). Chinese inscriptions by Muslim literati appear in mosques (e.g. Chen Si 陳思, *Laifu ming* 來復銘, Jinan, 1528). A poem in praise of the Prophet, the **"Hundred-Character Eulogy"** 百字讚, is attributed to Hongwu (text in §5). |

By the 16th century Chinese Muslims faced a crisis: Arabic and Persian learning was fading, and the community risked losing its religion while keeping only its customs. The Han Kitab grew out of the answer to that crisis.

## 3. The scripture-hall movement and the birth of the corpus

**Hu Dengzhou 胡登洲 (1522–1597)** of Weicheng/Xianyang in Shaanxi returned from study abroad (traditionally from the hajj) and founded **經堂教育** *jingtang jiaoyu*, "scripture-hall education": mosque colleges teaching Arabic and Persian texts, explained orally in Chinese. The curriculum was later fixed as the **"Thirteen Classics"** (十三本經), eight Arabic and five Persian works; about a third of them were Sufi.

Zhao Can's 趙燦 genealogy of the movement, *Jingxue xichuan pu* 經學系傳譜 (late 17th / early 18th century), reports that Hu "longed for the scriptures of our religion and wished to translate them into the national language, to be a law for this land for ten thousand generations", but did not complete it. His students' students did.

The movement split into regional schools: the **Shaanxi school** (Arabic, law and theology), the **Shandong school** (Jining; broad curriculum including Persian), and the **Nanjing/Jinling school**, where the leading Chinese-language writers worked. The first printed Han Kitab book is **Wang Daiyu's *Zhengjiao zhenquan*, 1642**.

## 5. Texts side by side: Chinese and English

**About this section.** The complete Han Kitab texts run to several hundred thousand characters (the *Tianfang xingli* alone has about 55,000), so a full parallel edition cannot fit in one report. The Chinese originals (1642–1903) are in the public domain; the existing English translations (Murata, Chittick and others) are modern and under copyright, so they are not reproduced. **The English translations below are new ones made for this report.** The Chinese is given as it appears in the cited sources; small variants between editions are noted. Section 10 lists where the full Chinese texts can be read.

| Chinese | English |
|---|---|
| 乾坤初始，天籍注名。 | When Heaven and Earth first began, his name was written in the heavenly register. |

| 受授天經，三十部冊， | He received the Heavenly Scripture, thirty sections in all, |
| 普化眾生。 | to transform all living beings. |
| 億兆君師，萬聖領袖。 | Lord and teacher of the countless multitudes, leader of ten thousand sages, |
| 協助天運，保庇國民。 | he aids the turning of Heaven and shelters the people of the realm. |
| 五時祈祐，默祝太平。 | Five times a day he prays for protection, silently wishing for great peace. |
| 存心真主，加志窮民。 | His heart is set on the True Lord; his will is turned to the poor. |
| 拯救患難，洞徹幽冥。 | He rescues those in distress; he sees through the hidden and the dark. |

*Note:* The poem survives in mosque inscriptions and later Hui collections; no Ming court record of it is known, and many historians treat the attribution as a later pious tradition. It is nonetheless the classic statement of the Hui claim that Islam had imperial approval.

### 5.3 Wang Daiyu, *Qingzhen daxue* 清真大學: the structure of the "Three Ones" 三一

Wang's treatise is built like the Confucian *Great Learning* (大學), with a "headline" chapter and three parts, each with three "grades" 三品.

| Chinese | Pinyin | English | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 題綱 | tígāng | Headline | Overview; the problem posed through the Eight Steps of the *Great Learning*. |
| **真一** | zhēn yī | **The Real One** | God (Allah), the One without second. |
| 本然 | běnrán | Original Suchness | God's Essence as it is in itself. |
| 本分 | běnfèn | Original Allotment | God's attributes. |
| 本為 | běnwéi | Original Activity | God's acts. |
| **數一** | shù yī | **The Numerical One** | The first created reality, from which the many proceed (equated with 無極 *wuji*; in Sufi terms the Muhammadan Reality). |
| 元勳 | yuánxūn | Primal Merit | The origin of creation. |
| 代理 | dàilǐ | Representative | The vicegerent (Ar. *khalīfa*). |
| 代書 | dàishū | Representative Book | The cosmos as God's written sign. |
| **體一** | tǐ yī | **The Embodied/Realized One** | The human being who realizes unity. |
| 知認 | zhīrèn | Recognition by knowledge | Knowing God through learning. |
| 見認 | jiànrèn | Recognition by seeing | Knowing God by direct witness. |
| 續認 | xùrèn | Recognition by continuation | Union that is sustained. |

The phrase used in Chinese scholarship to describe Wang's project is 以中土之漢文，展天方之奧義 — "to unfold the deep meanings of Arabia (*Tianfang*) in the Chinese writing of the Middle Land."

### 5.4 Liu Sanjie 劉三傑, *Qingzhen jiao shuo* 清真教說 (preserved in Liu Zhi's *Tianfang zhisheng shilu*)

| Chinese | English |
|---|---|
| 蓋未有天地萬物之先，惟有止一獨尊之真主， | Before Heaven, Earth and the ten thousand things existed, there was only the True Lord, one alone and uniquely honoured, |
| 運無極而開眾妙之門， | who set the Non-Ultimate (*wuji*) in motion and opened the gate of all wonders, |
| 用太極而造天地萬物之形。 | and used the Supreme Ultimate (*taiji*) to make the forms of Heaven, Earth and all things. |
| 〔經〕上窮造化之玄機，中闡人極之妙旨，下究物理之異同。 | [The scriptures] above, exhaust the hidden workings of creation; in the middle, set out the subtle meaning of the human ultimate; below, examine how the principles of things agree and differ. |
| 〔首務〕莫大於認識造化天地人神之真主； | [The first duty] is nothing greater than to recognize the True Lord who created Heaven, Earth, humans and spirits; |
| 認主之憑，則莫大乎拜主。 | and the proof of recognizing the Lord is nothing greater than worshipping the Lord. |
| 〔拜主之義〕求恕己身之罪業，默祝君上之太平， | [The meaning of worship]: to ask forgiveness for one's own sins, to pray silently for the peace of the sovereign, |
| 思親恩而感師訓，念友誼以及群生。 | to remember the kindness of parents and be grateful for teachers' instruction, and to keep friends and all living beings in mind. |
| 主之清：無所始也，而自不落於空無；無所終也，而更不拘於方位。 | The *purity* (清) of the Lord: He has no beginning, yet does not fall into emptiness; He has no end, and is still less bound by place. |
| 不受陰陽而超然自在，毫無干染而纖微自明。 | He is not subject to yin and yang but stands free and self-existent; untouched by any defilement, He is clear to the smallest detail. |

Note how worship of God is joined, in one sentence, to prayer for the emperor, gratitude to parents and teachers, and care for all beings: the Confucian social order is placed *inside* Islamic devotion.

### 5.5 Liu Zhi, *Tianfang xingli* 天方性理, *Benjing* 本經, Chapter 1 (opening)

Chapter 1 is titled 總述大世界造化流行之次第, "General account of the order in which the creation of the Great World unfolds."

| Chinese | English |
|---|---|
| 最初無稱， | At the very beginning there was no name; |
| 真體無著。 | the Real Substance had no attachment (was not yet manifest). |
| 惟茲實有， | Only this Real Being |
| 執一含萬。 | holds the One and contains the ten thousand. |
| 惟一含萬， | Because the One contains the ten thousand, |
| 妙用斯渾。 | its wondrous functions are wholly merged within it. |
| 惟體運用， | When the Substance sets its functions in motion, |
| 作為始出。 | activity first comes forth. |

*Variant:* some online transcriptions read 唯 for 惟 and 唯一 for 惟一; the meaning is the same.

**Summary of the rest of the chapter (paraphrase):** from this Real Being comes the first created principle, then primal *qi* 元氣, which divides into yin and yang; yin and yang produce fire, water, earth and air (*qi*) — the "four elements" 四行 of Greco-Islamic natural philosophy, replacing the Chinese Five Phases — which form heaven, earth, stars and seas; metal, wood and living beings are the "three offspring" 三子; together they make the "seven *xing*". Human beings alone receive the primal essence complete, so humanity is where creation reaches its fulfilment, and the return to God begins.

### 5.6 Preface to the *Tianfang xingli* (opening line)

| Chinese | English |
|---|---|
| 先天之學，何學也？ | What kind of learning is the "learning of what precedes Heaven" (*xiantian zhi xue*)? |
| 河未出圖，洛未出書，先天而天已弗違矣。 | Before the Yellow River brought forth its Chart and the Luo its Writing, [this learning] already went before Heaven, and Heaven did not oppose it. |

*Note:* the preface writer borrows the language of the *Book of Changes* (《易·文言》"先天而天弗違") to claim that the Islamic teaching on human nature belongs to the oldest stratum of Chinese wisdom. One online transcription reads "先天而大已不違"; the reading above follows the *Yijing* phrase it quotes.

### 5.7 Ma Zhu, *Qingzhen zhinan* 清真指南 (1683): two arguments, in English

Ma Zhu argues for a Creator by analogy with made things: why do drums not give birth to drums, and can a wooden horse neigh or a stone ox low? Things that cannot reproduce or act by themselves point to a maker beyond them. He also lists the natural abilities of animals — dragons soar, tigers bite, bulls gore, horses kick, silkworms spin, spiders weave, bees make honey — as signs that each creature was given its nature by God. On ethics, he holds that Islamic and Confucian loyalty 忠 and filiality 孝 are identical in content, but that Islam alone gives them their true root in the worship of the One.

## 7. Sinification and the Han Kitab: a history from the first publication to 2026

"Sinification" (漢化 *Hanhua*, "becoming Han") and "Sinicization" (中國化 *Zhongguohua*, "becoming Chinese") describe different things at different times. Three should be kept apart:

1. **Social and cultural acculturation** — Muslims adopting the Chinese language, surnames, dress and literary culture (Ming onwards).
2. **Intellectual synthesis** — Muslim scholars choosing to express Islam in Confucian terms (the Han Kitab, 1642 onwards). This was done *by* Muslims, *for* Muslims, to keep Islam alive.
3. **State policy** — governments directing how Islam must adapt (Ming marriage law; Qing legal distinctions; the PRC's "Sinicization of religion" from 2015).

### 7.1 Timeline

| Year | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 1368 | Ming founded; marriage law on Mongols and *semu*. | State-driven acculturation; Huihui become Chinese-speaking. |
| c. 1560s–1597 | Hu Dengzhou founds scripture-hall education. | Muslim response: preserve Islamic learning. |
| **1642** | **Wang Daiyu prints *Zhengjiao zhenquan* in Nanjing.** | **First Han Kitab book.** Islam explained in Chinese; argues Muslims can be loyal subjects (順主·順君·順親 — obey God, ruler, parents). |
| 1644–1645 | Ming falls to the Manchu Qing; Wang moves to Beijing. | Muslims serve on both sides; the corpus continues under Qing rule. |
| 1683 | Ma Zhu completes *Qingzhen zhinan* and seeks recognition at the Kangxi court. | Han Kitab used to petition the state. |
| 1704–1724 | Liu Zhi writes *Tianfang xingli*, *Tianfang dianli*, *Tianfang zhisheng shilu*. | Peak of the synthesis. |
| 1720s–1730s | Officials (e.g. in Shandong, Anhui) memorialize that Muslims are a separate, suspect sect; the **Yongzheng emperor rejects** proposals to ban Islam, saying Muslims are his subjects like any others, to be judged by conduct. | Imperial policy: tolerance of the religion, control of behaviour. Haiyun Ma calls the wider climate "Hui-phobia". |
| 1738 | Jin Tianzhu writes *Qingzhen shiyi* in Beijing to answer anti-Muslim prejudice. | Han Kitab as apologetic. |
| 1762 onward | Qing law adds special, harsher penalties for Muslims ("Hui") in some crimes. | Legal marking of Muslims as a category. |
| 1781–1784 | Jahriyya Sufi uprisings in Gansu (Salar and Hui); harsh repression, "New Teaching" banned. | Turning point: the Qing distinguish "good" (Sinified, loyal) Muslims from dangerous ones. |

| 1856–1873 | Panthay (Du Wenxiu) revolt in Yunnan. Ma Dexin first mediates, later sides with the Qing. | The Han Kitab tradition's last great scholar lives through the wars. |
| 1862–1877 | Northwest Muslim revolts; Zuo Zongtang's campaigns; massive loss of life and resettlement. | Hui–Han relations at their worst. |
| 1912 | Republic proclaimed; "Five Races Under One Union" 五族共和 names the Hui as one of the five peoples. | Hui as a nation-people, not only a religion. |
| 1910s–1930s | "Hui renaissance": Muslim schools (Chengda Normal School, 1925), journals, reprints of Han Kitab; the reformist Yihewani 伊赫瓦尼 movement pushes for Arabic scripture; Qurʾān translated (1927, 1932). Warlords such as Ma Fuxiang sponsor the Han Kitab. | The Han Kitab is valued as proof Muslims are Chinese patriots; reformers also seek Arabic sources over Confucian ones. |
| 1950s | PRC ethnic classification: Hui recognized as a *minzu* (民族). | Religion and ethnicity formally fused. |
| 1958–1976 | Religious reform campaigns; Cultural Revolution closes mosques; Shadian incident in Yunnan (1975). | Han Kitab study stops. |
| 1980s–2000s | Reform era: reprints (中國回族古籍叢書, 1980s–), *Qingzhen dadian* (2005), *Huizu diancang quanshu* (2008); international translation projects (Murata, Chittick, Tu Weiming). | Scholarly revival in China and abroad. |
| 2015 | Xi Jinping calls for "Sinicization" (中國化) of religions. | New state policy. |
| 2018–2022 | **Five-Year Outline on Persisting in the Sinicization of Islam** (2018–2022), adopted with the China Islamic Association; mosques required to display national flags and "core socialist values"; Arabic signs removed; Arab-style domes and minarets demolished or rebuilt in Chinese style across Ningxia, Gansu, Henan, Yunnan. | State-directed Sinicization, much broader than the Han Kitab's voluntary synthesis. |
| 2023 | The Central Institute of Socialism (United Front Work Department) holds a meeting in Ürümqi on implementing the plan in Xinjiang and promotes "using Confucianism to interpret scripture" — the Han Kitab method — as a model. | The Han Kitab is drawn into current state policy. Critics, including many Hui and Uyghur activists, say this turns a historic Muslim tradition into a tool of control. |

### 7.2 What the history shows

- **The Han Kitab began from below.** Wang Daiyu, Ma Zhu and Liu Zhi wrote to save Islamic knowledge among Muslims who had lost Arabic and Persian. They used Confucian language because it was the language of learning, not because the state required it.
- **It also served as a political argument.** By showing that Islam teaches loyalty to the ruler and filial piety, the authors argued that Muslims belonged in the empire. The 1782 Qianlong ruling shows the argument worked at the highest level.
- **It never meant giving up Islam.** Liu Zhi and Ma Zhu insist that only Islam knows the True Lord and that Confucianism lacks the knowledge of the Creator. The synthesis is "Islam in Chinese", not "Islam into Confucianism".
- **Modern state Sinicization is a different thing.** Since 2015 the PRC has used the Han Kitab as a historical precedent, but the 2018 plan concerns party leadership, architecture, education and supervision, imposed from above. Scholars such as David Stroup describe its aim as greater state supervision; some critics call it an erasure of Muslim identity.

## 8. Scholarly debates

1. **Is the Han Kitab Sinification?** Older Western and Chinese writing saw the Hui as "assimilated" Muslims. Lipman (*Familiar Strangers*, 1997) and Gladney (*Muslim Chinese*, 1991) showed that the Hui kept a distinct identity; Ben-Dor Benite (*The Dao of Muhammad*, 2005) argued that being Chinese and being Muslim were never opposites for the Han Kitab scholars, who saw themselves as Confucian literati *and* Muslim scholars.
2. **Confucian or Sufi?** Murata and Chittick show that the metaphysics is largely from the Persian Sufi tradition of Ibn ʿArabī's school (Jāmī, Nasafī, Rāzī), dressed in Neo-Confucian terms. Petersen (*Interpreting Islam in China*, 2017) traces how the tradition moved from Wang Daiyu's polemic through Liu Zhi's synthesis to Ma Dexin's re-Arabization.
3. **Was it read by non-Muslims?** It was written partly for Confucian readers, and some Han literati wrote prefaces, but the *Siku quanshu* editors dismissed Liu Zhi's *Tianfang dianli*. Most readers were Muslims.
4. **The 19th-century turn.** Ma Dexin and Ma Lianyuan, having studied in the Middle East, wrote in Arabic as well as Chinese and stressed direct access to Arabic sources. Republican-era reformers (Yihewani) sometimes criticized the Confucian vocabulary, while others (Ma Fuxiang, Hu Songshan) defended it.
5. **Current politics.** Whether the Han Kitab can be a model for Islam in today's China is contested: the state promotes it; Hui scholars use it to claim a legitimate place; critics see its use as cover for repression.
